Chapter 5 · Materials & the Environment

Asking a Language Model

Can GPT-4, Mixtral or a mechanics-tuned model choose materials the way experienced engineers do? 10 544 expert scores, 4 prompting strategies and two ways the models fail.

1Why ask a language model?

Chapters 1 to 4 chose materials from numbers: moduli, densities, eco-indicators, simulated stresses. Early in a design, the requirements are rarely numbers. They are words: a grip that should be lightweight, a component that must be corrosion resistant. Large language models (LLMs) read words, and have read a great deal about materials. Can they stand in for an experienced engineer at this stage?

Grandi and colleagues put that question to a test. They asked engineers to score nine material classes for sixteen design scenarios, asked three LLMs the same questions in four different ways, and measured how far the machines landed from the people. The paper asks two questions:

  1. To what extent do LLMs show bias toward particular materials?
  2. Which prompting methods steer an LLM toward expert-like material choices?

This chapter retells the study with its complete data. Every figure below is computed in your browser from the survey responses and model answers released with the paper, and the headline numbers match the paper's tables.

2The experiment

The expert data

The survey crossed four designs (kitchen utensil grip, spacecraft component, underwater component, safety helmet) with four criteria (lightweight, heat resistant, corrosion resistant, high strength). For each of the 16 scenarios, respondents scored nine broad material classes: steel, aluminium, titanium, glass, wood, thermoplastic, elastomer, thermoset and composite. Keeping only respondents with material-selection experience gives 10 544 scores.The paper reports 136 respondents with material-selection experience. The released processing script keeps the 117 respondents who answered "Yes" to the familiarity question and downsamples the over-represented kitchen-grip/lightweight scenario to 800 answers. That pipeline gives exactly 10 544 scores and the paper's tables, and it is the one used here.

The models

  • GPT-4 (gpt-4-0125-preview), a closed model with strong benchmark results, default temperature 0.1.
  • Mixtral (mixtral-8x7b-instruct-v0.1), an open-weights mixture-of-experts model, default temperature 0.75.
  • MechGPT, a 13-billion-parameter model fine-tuned on mechanics and materials text, default temperature 0.4.

Four ways to ask

Every prompt starts with the same preamble. What follows it changes with the strategy. Pick a strategy and a scenario to see exactly what the model was sent.

Two measures of agreement

For one scenario and material, let the expert scores have mean \mu and standard deviation \sigma. The z-score of a model answer \hat y says how many expert standard deviations it sits above or below the expert mean, and the mean absolute error (MAE) averages its distance to each of the n expert answers y_i:

z = \frac{\hat y - \mu}{\sigma}, \qquad \text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \lvert y_i - \hat y \rvert .

Both are averaged over the 144 questions. A mean z near zero means no systematic over- or under-scoring; a small MAE means the answers sit close to individual experts. They can disagree: a model that is right on average but never commits can have a good z and a poor MAE.

4What the models got right and wrong

Every model scores too high

Across all experiments the mean z-score is positive for every model and almost every strategy, and the MAE sits around 3 points on a 0–10 scale. The models rate materials as more suitable than experts do, by a large margin.

The z-score and the MAE do not crown the same winner. By z, MechGPT looks best, partly because its parallel answers contain many zeros that pull its mean below the experts'. By MAE, GPT-4 is closest to individual experts. The parallel prompt gives the lowest MAE averaged over the three models (3.045) and the best z for GPT-4 and Mixtral. It also cuts the number of prompts from 144 to 16 and GPT-4's run time from about 3 minutes to 35 seconds. Few-shot prompting, with two worked examples, did not help on average. Chain-of-thought was the worst strategy for GPT-4 and Mixtral and multiplied GPT-4's run time from 3 to 57 minutes, although its written reasoning may help a designer more than a bare number.

Failure mode 1: overconfidence in some materials

Look at which materials the models over-score. With the zero-shot prompt, GPT-4 gives elastomer a score 6.2 points above the expert mean for a lightweight kitchen utensil grip, and wood 5.5 points too much for a lightweight underwater component. It also under-scores: steel for a lightweight safety helmet sits 4.6 points below the experts.

Failure mode 2: too little variation

Experts change their minds with the scenario. Titanium is excellent for a spacecraft component and pointless for a kitchen grip; wood is fine for a grip and absurd for a heat-resistant spacecraft part. The models move much less. Mixtral gives titanium a 9 in almost every scenario. Across all scenarios and materials, expert scores have a standard deviation of 3.35, against 2.49, 2.49 and 2.93 for GPT-4, Mixtral and MechGPT. The models respond to the material name more than to the design question.

Temperature hardly matters

Temperature controls how randomly an LLM picks its next token. Sweeping it from 0 to 1 changes neither z-score nor MAE in any meaningful way, so the vendors' defaults are adequate for this task.

5Limitations and what comes next

  • Explainability. The study scores materials without asking why, apart from chain-of-thought, whose reasoning was not evaluated. A user study could test whether explanations help designers more than scores do.
  • Simple scenarios. Each scenario has one criterion. Real selection weighs several conflicting criteria, as in the value functions of Chapter 1.
  • Experts as ground truth. Expert scores vary widely and carry their own biases.
  • Prompting and models. Only four strategies and three models were tested. Combining parallel prompting with few-shot examples or chain-of-thought, retrieval-augmented generation over a materials database, or fine-tuning on selection data rather than on failure mechanics are natural next steps.

6Where this sits in the book

The earlier chapters choose materials from properties and a stated objective; the answer is traceable to a slope, an index or a Pareto front. An LLM chooses from text and from what it has read, and the answer is not traceable at all. The two are complementary. A language model can turn a vague brief into candidate classes and criteria, which the property-based tools of Chapters 1–4 can then rank and check. On its own, this study shows, it is overconfident and insensitive to context, and it should not be trusted to make the choice.
